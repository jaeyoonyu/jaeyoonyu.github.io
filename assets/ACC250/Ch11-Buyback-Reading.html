Stock Buybacks: Fewer Shares, Bigger Slices

Read before class. No prior knowledge needed; each idea builds on the one before it.

1. A share is a slice of the company

A corporation divides its ownership into equal pieces called shares. If you own some of the shares, you own that fraction of the company: a fraction of its profit and a fraction of its shareholders’ equity (SHE), which is what would be left for the owners if the company sold its assets and paid off its liabilities (SHE = A − L).

Figure 1. Owning 1 of 10 shares means owning 10% of the company.

2. Four ways to count shares

Authorized: the most shares the board of directors allows the company to issue.

Issued: shares the company has sold to investors at some point.

Treasury: issued shares the company has bought back and now holds itself.

Outstanding: issued shares still held by shareholders. Outstanding = Issued − Treasury.

Figure 2. The Ch11 example: 1M authorized, 700K issued, 100K repurchased ⇒ 600K outstanding.

Only outstanding shares count as owners. Treasury shares get no vote, no dividends and no slice of the profit: the company cannot own a piece of itself.

3. What is a buyback?

In a stock buyback (also called a share repurchase), the company uses its own cash to buy its shares from investors who want to sell. The shares become treasury stock.

Figure 3. Cash goes out to the sellers; the shares come back to the company.

4. The accounting: cash down, SHE down, no expense

EX. A company pays $100 cash to buy back its own shares.

Treasury stock is a contra-equity account: it is subtracted from SHE. It is not an asset and not an expense, so net income does not change (see Figure 4).

Figure 4. A buyback shrinks both sides of the balance sheet by the cash paid.

5. Fewer shares ⇒ higher earnings per share (EPS)

EPS = Net income ÷ Average shares outstanding.* It tells you how much profit each slice earns. A buyback does not change net income (the pizza), but there are fewer slices, so each slice gets bigger.

Figure 5. Same profit, fewer shares, higher EPS.

EX. Net income $100K; 50K shares outstanding. The company buys back 10K shares.

6. Is a bigger slice always good?

Not automatically. The cash paid to sellers is gone, so the company owns less. Remaining owners win only if the price paid is below what the shares are really worth. That is why firms tend to buy back when they think their stock price is low and they have more cash than good investments. In the news: Berkshire Hathaway, holding about $377 billion of cash, bought back $9.6 billion of its stock in Q3 2026, more than double the prior quarter.†
