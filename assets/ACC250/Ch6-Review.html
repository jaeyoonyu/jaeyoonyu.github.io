    Inventory vs supplies

    Q1

    A sporting goods store buys tennis rackets to resell to customers and printer paper for its own office. Which statement is correct?

    1. AThe rackets are Inventory until they are sold; the paper is Supplies until it is used.✔
    2. BThe rackets are Supplies until they are used; the paper is Inventory until it is sold.✘
    3. CBoth are recorded as Inventory, because both were bought from suppliers.✘
    4. DBoth are recorded as expenses when they are bought.✘
    5. EThe rackets are recorded as Cost of Goods Sold when they are bought; the paper is recorded as Supplies.✘

    A. Both are current assets bought from suppliers, and they differ only in what turns them into an expense. Inventory is goods bought but not yet sold, so the rackets become Cost of Goods Sold when a customer buys them. Supplies are goods bought but not yet used, so the paper becomes Supplies Expense as the office uses it.

    Perpetual inventory system

    Q2

    Under the perpetual inventory system, which statement is correct?

    1. AInventory is counted only at the end of the period, and Cost of Goods Sold is worked out from the count.✘
    2. BCost of Goods Sold is recorded once, at the end of the year.✘
    3. CInventory purchases are recorded as an expense when they are bought.✘
    4. DInventory does not appear on the balance sheet until it has been counted.✘
    5. EInventory and Cost of Goods Sold are updated every time inventory is bought or sold.✔

    E. The perpetual system updates Inventory with every purchase, return and sale, and records Cost of Goods Sold at each sale, so both balances are known at every moment. A describes the periodic system, where Cost of Goods Sold is inferred from the inventory equation after the count.

    Inventory equation

    Q3

    A shop that counts its inventory at the end of each month began June with $4,000 of inventory, bought $15,000 more during June, and counted $3,000 on hand at the end of June. What was its Cost of Goods Sold for June?

    1. A$8,000✘
    2. B$14,000✘
    3. C$16,000✔
    4. D$19,000✘
    5. E$22,000✘

    C. Goods available for sale are $4,000 + $15,000 = $19,000. Whatever is not still on the shelf was sold: $19,000 − $3,000 = $16,000. $19,000 (D) forgets to take out the ending inventory.

    Payment terms · 2/10, n/30

    Q4

    On March 3 a company bought $8,000 of inventory on account with terms 2/10, n/30. It paid the invoice on March 11. How much cash did it pay?

    1. A$6,400✘
    2. B$7,840✔
    3. C$7,920✘
    4. D$8,000✘
    5. E$8,160✘

    B. 2/10 means a 2% discount for paying within 10 days, and March 11 is 8 days after the purchase, so it qualifies: $8,000 × 98% = $7,840. Paying after March 13 would have cost the full $8,000 (D), due by day 30.

    Single-step vs multi-step

    Q5

    A company prepares both a single-step and a multi-step income statement for the same year. Which amount appears, and is the same, on both?

    1. AGross Profit.✘
    2. BIncome from Operations.✘
    3. CIncome before Income Tax.✘
    4. DNet Income.✔
    5. ENone of these; the two statements report different results.✘

    D. The two formats list the same revenues and expenses and arrive at the same Net Income. What the multi-step statement adds is the subtotals on the way down, Gross Profit, Income from Operations and Income before Income Tax, none of which a single-step statement shows.

    Gross profit

    Q6

    Pinecone Goods reported Net Sales of $60,000, Cost of Goods Sold of $36,000, and SG&A expenses of $14,000. What is its gross profit?

    1. A$10,000✘
    2. B$24,000✔
    3. C$36,000✘
    4. D$46,000✘
    5. E$60,000✘

    B. Gross profit is Net Sales less Cost of Goods Sold: $60,000 − $36,000 = $24,000. SG&A is subtracted only below gross profit; taking it out as well gives $10,000 (A), income from operations.

    Gross profit ratio

    Q7

    A retailer reported Net Sales of $80,000, gross profit of $28,000 and net income of $8,000. What is its gross profit ratio?

    1. A10%✘
    2. B28.6%✘
    3. C35%✔
    4. D65%✘
    5. E90%✘

    C. Gross profit ratio = Gross Profit ÷ Net Sales = $28,000 ÷ $80,000 = 35%: for every $1 of sales, 35 cents is left after the cost of the goods sold. Net income ÷ Net Sales, 10% (A), is the net profit ratio.

    Q8 · Transaction A — Bikes bought on account

    Q8

    Transaction A. Walmart received $10,500 of bikes it bought from a supplier on account. Record transaction A.

    Account choices

    1. 1Accounts Payable
    2. 2Accounts Receivable
    3. 3Cash
    4. 4Cost of Goods Sold
    5. 5Inventory
    6. 6Retained Earnings
    7. 7Sales Revenue
    8. 8Supplies
    9. 9Supplies Expense
    10. 10Transportation Expense
    AccountDebitCredit
    5. Inventory (+A)10,500
    1. Accounts Payable (+L)10,500

    5, then 1. The bikes are goods bought to resell and not yet sold, so they go into Inventory, an asset, at their cost. Nothing is paid yet, so Accounts Payable records what is owed. No expense: nothing has been sold.

    Q9 · Transaction B — Paid for delivery

    Q9

    Transaction B. Walmart paid a trucking company $200 cash to deliver those bikes to its store. Record transaction B.

    Account choices

    1. 1Accounts Payable
    2. 2Accounts Receivable
    3. 3Cash
    4. 4Cost of Goods Sold
    5. 5Inventory
    6. 6Retained Earnings
    7. 7Sales Revenue
    8. 8Supplies
    9. 9Supplies Expense
    10. 10Transportation Expense
    AccountDebitCredit
    5. Inventory (+A)200
    3. Cash (−A)200

    5, then 3. Under the cost principle, the cost of getting goods to the store is part of what they cost, so the $200 is added to Inventory. Transportation Expense (10) is the trap: the freight becomes an expense only later, inside Cost of Goods Sold, when the bikes are sold.

    1, then 5. The returned bikes leave the store, so Inventory goes down, and the supplier cuts the bill, so Accounts Payable goes down by the same $500. No cash moves.

    Q11 · Transaction D — Sale — the revenue

    Q11

    Transaction D. Walmart sold four mountain bikes for $200 each, $800 in total, and the customers paid cash. Walmart had recorded these bikes in Inventory at $175 each, a total cost of $700. Record the selling price of transaction D.

    Account choices

    1. 1Accounts Payable
    2. 2Accounts Receivable
    3. 3Cash
    4. 4Cost of Goods Sold
    5. 5Inventory
    6. 6Retained Earnings
    7. 7Sales Revenue
    8. 8Supplies
    9. 9Supplies Expense
    10. 10Transportation Expense
    AccountDebitCredit
    3. Cash (+A)800
    7. Sales Revenue (+R, +SHE)800

    3, then 7. The bikes were delivered and the customers paid, so Cash goes up and Sales Revenue is recorded at the $800 selling price. This is the first of the two entries a sale needs.

    Q12 · Transaction D — Sale — the cost

    Q12

    Transaction D. Walmart sold four mountain bikes for $200 each, $800 in total, and the customers paid cash. Walmart had recorded these bikes in Inventory at $175 each, a total cost of $700. Record the cost of the bikes sold in transaction D.

    Account choices

    1. 1Accounts Payable
    2. 2Accounts Receivable
    3. 3Cash
    4. 4Cost of Goods Sold
    5. 5Inventory
    6. 6Retained Earnings
    7. 7Sales Revenue
    8. 8Supplies
    9. 9Supplies Expense
    10. 10Transportation Expense
    AccountDebitCredit
    4. Cost of Goods Sold (+E, −SHE)700
    5. Inventory (−A)700

    4, then 5. The bikes that left the store cost $700, so Inventory goes down by their cost and the same amount becomes Cost of Goods Sold, matched to the $800 of revenue it helped earn. The entry uses cost, not the selling price.

    The point One sale, two entries: revenue at the selling price, and Cost of Goods Sold at what the goods cost.

    Q13 · Inventory after the four transactions

    Q13

    What is Walmart’s Inventory balance for bikes after transactions A to D?

    The four transactions

    • A — Received $10,500 of bikes bought on account.
    • B — Paid $200 cash to have the bikes delivered.
    • C — Returned $500 of bikes to the supplier.
    • D — Sold bikes costing $700 for $800 cash.

    Inventory — ending balance

    Amount: $9500

    $9,500. Inventory went up by A’s $10,500 and B’s $200 of delivery, and down by C’s $500 return and the $700 cost of the bikes sold in D: $10,500 + $200 − $500 − $700 = $9,500, a debit balance.

    Q14 · Gross profit

    Q14

    What is Walmart’s gross profit from transactions A to D?

    The four transactions

    • A — Received $10,500 of bikes bought on account.
    • B — Paid $200 cash to have the bikes delivered.
    • C — Returned $500 of bikes to the supplier.
    • D — Sold bikes costing $700 for $800 cash.

    Gross profit

    Amount: $100

    $100. Net Sales are D’s $800, and Cost of Goods Sold is the $700 the sold bikes cost: $800 − $700 = $100. The $200 of delivery is not subtracted again; it sits in the cost of the bikes, sold or unsold.

    The point Gross profit = Net Sales − Cost of Goods Sold. What the goods cost reaches the income statement only when they are sold, not when they are bought or paid for.

    Inventory is goods bought but not yet sold; when it is sold, its cost becomes Cost of Goods Sold.
    Under the perpetual system every purchase, transportation cost and return updates Inventory, and every sale records Cost of Goods Sold.
    Gross profit is Net Sales less Cost of Goods Sold; the gross profit ratio divides it by Net Sales.
