    ACC 250 · Ch8 Receivables and Bad Debts

    What if the customer never pays?

    How Crocs and Costco report bad debts in their 10-K filings.

    Crocs: Form 10-K, year ended Dec 31, 2024 ($ thousands) · Costco: Form 10-K

    The problem

    Selling on credit has a cost

    1
    A shoe store orders $100,000 of Crocs and promises to pay in 60 days. Crocs records revenue and an account receivable.
    2
    Some stores will close or fall behind, and some of that money will never be collected.
    3
    Crocs doesn’t know which customers will not pay, or when it will find out.
    Think about it

    Should Crocs record the loss now, in the year of the sale, or later, when a customer actually fails to pay?

    The big idea

    The expense comes first, the write-off later

    When the expense is recorded

    When Crocs estimates the loss, in the same period as the sale. That is the matching principle.

    When a customer finally defaults

    The write-off only moves the amount out of AR and out of the allowance. No new expense, and net AR does not change.

    Answer to slide 2Record the loss now, as an estimate. That is the allowance method, and it is what GAAP requires.

    Crocs · Putting it in perspective

    How big are Crocs’s bad debts?

    Bad debt expense vs. revenue

    $1.35 million on about $4.1 billion of revenue: roughly 3 cents per $100 of sales.

    Allowance vs. gross receivables

    12,649 ÷ 289,236 ≈ 4.4% of what customers owe is expected never to be collected.

    Small, but it is an estimate that management makes every quarter. A bigger allowance means lower net income today.

    Costco · The contrast

    Why Costco has almost no bad debts

    Members pay at the register: cash, debit or credit card. If a member never pays the credit card bill, that is the bank’s loss, not Costco’s.
    Costco’s receivables ($3.2 billion at Aug 31, 2025) are mostly owed by vendors (rebates), pharmacy insurers and credit card partners.

    What Costco’s 10-K says

    Receivables are recorded net of an allowance, and the allowance was immaterial, too small to matter to investors.

    Key ideaBad debts matter when a company sells on credit to other businesses (Crocs to shoe stores), not when customers pay on the spot.

    Ch8 Concepts · Review

    Allowance method vs. direct write-off

    Allowance method (GAAP)

    Estimate uncollectible accounts at the end of each period. Record bad debt expense and the allowance then. Matches expense with the revenue it helped earn. Reports AR at net realizable value.

    Direct write-off method

    Record bad debt expense only when a specific account is written off. Simple, but violates matching and overstates AR. Allowed only when bad debts are immaterial (and used for taxes).

    Net realizable valueAccounts receivable − Allowance for doubtful accounts = the amount expected to be collected.

    Write-offDr Allowance for doubtful accounts / Cr Accounts receivableNo change to net AR or net income
    RecoveryReverse the write-off, then Dr Cash / Cr Accounts receivableNo change to net income

    Bad debt expense is usually reported within selling, general and administrative expenses.

    Ch8 Concepts · Review

    Estimating the allowance

    Aging of receivables

    Group AR by how long it has been outstanding. Apply a higher uncollectible % to older groups. The total is the ending balance the allowance should have.

    Adjusting entry amount

    Bad debt expense = required ending balance − balance already in the allowance (add it back if the allowance has a debit balance).

    Allowance for doubtful accounts (T-account logic)
    Beginning balance+
    + Bad debt expense (estimate)+
    − Write-offs−
    = Ending balance=

    Ch8 Concepts · Review

    Receivables turnover and notes receivable

    Receivables turnover

    = Net credit sales ÷ Average net AR
    Days to collect = 365 ÷ turnover

    Crocs: 4,102 ÷ [(258 + 306) ÷ 2] ≈ 14.6 times, about 25 days. (Uses total revenue, which includes cash sales in its own stores, so true days to collect are longer.)

    Notes receivable

    A written promise to pay, usually with interest.
    Interest = Face value × Annual rate × Fraction of the year

    Accrue interest earned but not yet collected at year-end: Dr Interest receivable / Cr Interest revenue.
